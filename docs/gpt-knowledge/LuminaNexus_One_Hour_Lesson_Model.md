# LuminaNexus One-Hour Lesson Model

## Program Phrase

**Hebrew for Every Grade, Every Beginning.**

## Purpose

LuminaNexus lessons are designed for a one-hour-per-week Hebrew enrichment model serving K–12 students.

Each lesson should be simple, flexible, confidence-building, and appropriate for mixed-age or mixed-readiness groups.

The goal is not to overwhelm students with too much Hebrew at once. The goal is to help each student take one clear step forward.

## Core Lesson Principle

Every one-hour lesson should include:

1. A warm beginning
2. A small review
3. One new Hebrew concept
4. Guided practice
5. A joyful activity
6. Reading or writing practice
7. A simple closing step

Students should leave each lesson feeling that Hebrew is approachable, meaningful, and possible.

## Standard One-Hour Lesson Structure

### 1. Welcome and Review — 5 Minutes

Begin with a warm greeting and a quick review.

Possible review activities:

* Show a Hebrew letter from the previous lesson
* Ask students to trace a letter in the air
* Review right-to-left directionality
* Ask students to identify a familiar Hebrew word
* Sing or chant a short review phrase
* Let students share one thing they remember

The purpose is to help students re-enter Hebrew gently.

### 2. New Hebrew Concept — 10 Minutes

Introduce one clear concept.

Examples:

* One new Hebrew letter
* A pair of letters
* Hebrew reads from right to left
* A basic vowel sound
* A simple word
* A student’s name in Hebrew letters
* A root idea
* A cultural word or phrase

Keep this portion focused. Do not introduce too many new ideas at once.

A good lesson gives students one doorway, not ten hallways.

### 3. Guided Practice — 15 Minutes

Practice the new concept together.

Possible activities:

* Letter recognition cards
* Matching Hebrew letters to sounds
* Tracing letters
* Reading simple syllables
* Building a word from letters
* Saying a sound aloud as a group
* Finding a letter inside a word
* Teacher-led decoding

The teacher should model first, then invite students to try.

Mistakes should be treated as part of learning, not failure.

### 4. Game, Song, Name Activity, or Movement — 10 Minutes

Use a joyful activity to help the lesson settle.

Possible activities:

* Hebrew letter scavenger hunt
* Letter matching game
* Name-building activity
* Hebrew sound clapping
* Movement for right-to-left reading
* Simple song
* Partner practice
* Whiteboard challenge

For younger students, use movement, color, rhythm, and visual play.

For older students, use pattern recognition, names, roots, culture, and meaning.

The activity should support the lesson, not distract from it.

### 5. Reading or Writing Practice — 10 Minutes

Give students a quiet or semi-quiet practice step.

Possible activities:

* Trace the Hebrew letter
* Copy a simple word
* Circle a letter in a word bank
* Read a syllable aloud
* Match a Hebrew word to a picture
* Write one Hebrew letter from memory
* Decode a short word
* Build a name from Hebrew letters

Students should experience a small success before the lesson ends.

### 6. Reflection and Take-Home Step — 10 Minutes

Close the lesson by helping students name what they learned.

Possible reflection questions:

* What letter did we learn today?
* What sound did we practice?
* What Hebrew word did you recognize?
* What felt easier by the end of class?
* What is one thing you can show someone at home?

Take-home steps should be short and realistic.

Examples:

* Practice writing Aleph three times
* Show someone which way Hebrew reads
* Find today’s Hebrew letter on the worksheet
* Say the sound of the letter aloud
* Review one word from class

The closing should leave students encouraged.

## Mixed-Age Classroom Use

LuminaNexus lessons may serve students of different ages and readiness levels in the same room.

A mixed-age lesson should include:

* A shared opening
* One whole-group concept
* Track-based practice
* A whole-group activity
* A simple closing

For example, all students may learn the letter Mem together.

Aleph Track students identify Mem.

Bet Track students read Mem with a vowel.

Gimel Track students find Mem inside a short word or root.

This allows every student to participate without being embarrassed.

## Lesson Design for Aleph Track

Aleph Track lessons should focus on first contact with Hebrew.

Use:

* Large letters
* Clear sounds
* Visual recognition
* Names
* Repetition
* Tracing
* Simple words
* Encouragement

Avoid rushing into vowels too quickly.

The main goal is confidence and familiarity.

## Lesson Design for Bet Track

Bet Track lessons should help students build words.

Use:

* Letter review
* Basic vowels
* Syllables
* Sound blending
* Simple decoding
* Word families
* Short vocabulary lists
* Reading practice

The main goal is moving from letters into syllables and words.

## Lesson Design for Gimel Track

Gimel Track lessons should help students read with meaning.

Use:

* Short words
* Simple phrases
* Roots
* Vocabulary families
* Cultural context
* Guided reading
* Interpretation
* Reading aloud

The main goal is helping students understand that Hebrew words carry structure, memory, and meaning.

## Teacher Tone

Teachers should be:

* Warm
* Patient
* Clear
* Encouraging
* Respectful
* Age-aware
* Flexible

Teachers should avoid making students feel behind.

The guiding sentence is:

**You are not late. This is your beginning.**
